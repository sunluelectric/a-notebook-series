\chapter{Digital Signal Processing}

Digital signal processing is the basis of computer controlled system, and the later is one of the most important use cases of the former.

This chapter servers as a brief introduction to digital signal processing, including commonly seen discrete signal and digital system features, $z$-transform and frequency domain analysis of discrete signals and digital systems, signal sampling and reconstruction, discrete Fourier transform and digital filtering.

\section{Digital Versus Analog Systems}

It has become a common practice nowadays to use digital controllers over analog controllers as the preferable choice in most occasions. A commonly used block diagram of a digital processing system looks like Fig. \ref{fig:digital_system}. The digital and analog modules are given by the black and the red portions respectively. The \myabb{analog-to-digital converter}{ADC} and the \myabb{digital-to-analog converter}{DAC} bridges the digital and analog worlds, and they are colored in orange.
\begin{figure}[!htb]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{chapters/part-2/figures/digital_system.jpg}
	\caption{A block diagram of a digital system.} \label{fig:digital_system}
\end{figure}

A digital processing system has the following advantages over a conventional analog processing system.
\begin{itemize}
	\item More flexible. Digital processor is usually re-programmable, thus making it cheaper and faster to scale than an analog system.
	\item Easily reproducible. With standard hardware setup, reproducing a digital system can be as simple as copy-and-paste the code and it is much easier than re-building an analog system. 
	\item Consistent performance. The precision, accuracy, responding time, etc., of a digital system is more deterministic and consistent than an analog system.
\end{itemize}

However, digital system is not always an superior alternative to the counterpart analog system. Comparing with an analog system, a digital system may have the following drawbacks.

Due to the use of ADC, DAC, \myabb{digital signature processor}{DSP}, etc., a digital system can introduce a ``capital cost''. For very simple functions that can be realized with a small-scale analog circuit, using digital system could be more expensive. ADC, DAC, DSP have fixed computation cycle and input-output bandwidth. Though the development trend suggests that these units are becoming faster and faster, there are still tasks where the speed and the bandwidth of a digital system is too slow. In such cases, analog system or optical system might be a better solution.

\section{Basic Concepts}

Basic concepts and categories of digital signals and digital systems are introduced.

\subsection{Channel and Dimension}

Signals, both analog and digital, can be scalar or vector. A signal is often formulated as a function of time, location, etc. The number components in the signal and the number of independent variables formulating the signal are knows as the \mync{signal channel} and \mync{signal dimension} respectively. A signal with vector size $n$ and number of independent variables $m$ is known as a $n$-channel-$m$-dimensional signal. An example is given below.

Consider a colored image
\begin{eqnarray}
	I(x,y) &=& \left[\begin{array}{c}
		I_r(x,y) \\
		I_g(x,y) \\
		I_b(x,y)
	\end{array}\right] \nonumber
\end{eqnarray}
where $I_r$, $I_g$, $I_b$ and $(x,y)$ are the RGBs and the coordinate of the pixels respectively. This is a $3$-channel-$2$-dimensional signal.

Though multi-channel-multi-dimensional signals are very common, for the convenience of study, this chapter will mostly focus on $1$-channel-$1$-dimensional signals.

\subsection{Continuous, Discrete and Digital Signals}

Consider a signal formulated as a function of time. If the signal is continuous in both the time scale and value scale, it is known as a \mync{continuous signal}. Most signals in our daily life, such as the speed of a car, can be modeled by a continuous signal.

Consider sampling a continuous signal by recording its values in countable and discrete timestamps. The time scale of the signal becomes discrete and countable. A signal of such type is known as a \mync{discrete-time signal}, or sometimes \mync{discrete signal} for short.

Consider mapping the value of a continuous signal to a set of discrete values, usually values with finite digits. The value scale of the signal becomes discrete countable. A signal of such type is known as a \mync{discrete-valued signal}.

A signal that is both discrete-time and discrete-valued, i.e., has both discrete time and value scales, is also known as a \mync{digital signal}. 

A figure to illustrate the relation of the above signals is given in Fig. \ref{fig:signal_types}.

\subsection{Deterministic and Random Signals}

There is always random noise and stochastic disturbances in a physical system. From this point of view, no system or signal is deterministic. In practice, as long as the signal is precise and accurate enough for the usage, it is considered a deterministic signal. Otherwise if the signal is unknown or not accurate enough, it is considered a random signal.

Deterministic signals and models are relatively easier to analyze. When it comes to random signals and stochastic systems, probability and statistics based analysis are usually adopted.

\section{Important Signals and Features}

Commonly seen elementary digital signals and their features are introduced. Notice that similar counterparts and features apply to continuous signals as well, but they are not discussed in this section.

\subsection{Elementary Signals}

Let $n$ be the time index. The unit sample sequence is given by
\begin{eqnarray}
	\delta(n) &=& \left\{\begin{array}{cc}
		1 & n = 0 \\
		0 & \textup{otherwise}
	\end{array}\right. \nonumber
\end{eqnarray}
The unit step signal is given by
\begin{eqnarray}
	u(n) &=& \left\{\begin{array}{cc}
		1 & n \geq 0 \\
		0 & \textup{otherwise}
	\end{array}\right. \nonumber
\end{eqnarray}
The unit ramp signal is given by 
\begin{eqnarray}
	u_r(n) &=& \left\{\begin{array}{cc}
		n & n \geq 0 \\
		0 & \textup{otherwise}
	\end{array}\right. \nonumber
\end{eqnarray}
The exponential signal is given by
\begin{eqnarray}
	x(n) &=& a^n \nonumber
\end{eqnarray}
where notice that $a$ can be a complex value denoted by $a=re^{j\theta}$, in which case
\begin{eqnarray}
	x(n) = r^ne^{j\theta n} = r^n\left(\textup{cos}(\theta n) + j\textup{sin}(\theta n)\right) \nonumber
\end{eqnarray}

\subsection{Energy and Power of a Signal}

The \mync{energy of a signal} and the \mync{power of a signal} can be calculated by
\begin{eqnarray}
	E &=& \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} |x(n)|^2 \nonumber \\
	P &=& \lim_{N\rightarrow\infty} \dfrac{1}{2N+1}\sum_{n=-N}^{N}|x(n)|^2 \nonumber
\end{eqnarray}
respectively. A signal with finite energy is known as an \mync{energy signal}. A signal with finite power is known as a \mync{power signal}. From the definition, we know that an energy signal must have a power of zero. Examples are given in Fig. \ref{fig:energypowersignal}.

	\caption{Example of energy and power signals, where the top plot shows an energy signal, the middle a power signal, and the bottom neither.} \label{fig:energypowersignal}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Periodic Signal}

A signal $x(n)$ is a \mync{periodic signal} if
\begin{eqnarray}
	x(n) &=& x(n + N), \textup{~for all~} n \nonumber
\end{eqnarray}
and the smallest $N>0$ is the \mync{period} of the signal. Notice that a constant signal $x(n)=c$ is often taken as a periodic signal as well, but of non-definable period. In the remaining of our discussion, we shall consider the ``regular'' periodic signal with $N>0$ unless otherwise mentioned.

It can be proved easily that a period signal has finite power as long as $x(n)$ is bounded. The energy, on the other hand, is infinite unless $x(n)=0$ is a zero signal.

\subsection{Symmetric and Antisymmetric Signals}

A signal $x(n)$ is a \mync{symmetric signal} (also known as \mync{even signal}) if
\begin{eqnarray}
	x(n) &=& x(-n) \nonumber
\end{eqnarray}
A signal $x(n)$ is an \mync{asymmetric signal} (also known as \mync{odd signal}) if
\begin{eqnarray}
	x(n) &=& -x(-n) \nonumber
\end{eqnarray}

Given any signal $x(n)$, define
\begin{eqnarray}
	x_e(n) &=& \dfrac{1}{2}\left[x(n) + x(-n)\right] \nonumber \\
	x_o(n) &=& \dfrac{1}{2}\left[x(n) - x(-n)\right] \nonumber
\end{eqnarray}
It can be easily verified that $x_e(n)$ and $x_o(n)$ are even and odd signals respectively, and $x(n) = x_e(n) + x_o(n)$. Therefore, we can conclude that any signal can be decomposed into the symmetric and the antisymmetric portions.

\subsection{Signal Transformation}

For a general discrete signal $x(n)$, $x(-n)$ is a fold of the signal at $n=0$. We can easily prove that $x(-n+2k)$ is a fold of the signal at $n=k$.

Signal $x(n-k)$ is a shift of the original signal $x(n)$ to the right by $k$ samples, i.e., it is $x(n)$ delayed by $k$ samples. Likewise, $x(n+k)$ is the original signal to shift to the left.

Signal $x(\mu n)$, $\mu \in \mathbb{N}^+$ is a time-scaling or down-sampling of the original signal $x(n)$. Graphical wise, it ``squeezes''. A demonstrative figure is given in Fig. \ref{fig:downsampling}. The values captured by the blue circle is the down sampled signal sequence. In this example, $\mu=2$, i.e., one out of two samples are captured.

\section{Digital System}

A digital system maps discrete signal $x(n)$ to another discrete signal $y(n)$. It can be described by
\begin{eqnarray}
	y(n) &=& \mathcal{T}[x(n)] \nonumber
\end{eqnarray}
in general. The system is denoted by $\mathcal{T}$.

\subsection{Input-Output Model}

There are varieties of ways to model the system. A commonly used way is to use an input-output model described by the difference equation. A simple example is given below.
\begin{eqnarray}
	y(k) = \sum_{i=-\infty}^{k-1}x(i) = y(k-1) + x(k-1) \label{eq:diffrenceeqexp}
\end{eqnarray}
Equation \eqref{eq:diffrenceeqexp} calculates the aggregated sum of the input signal. For this system, we can see that its output $y(k)$ can be entirely described by its previous value $y(k-1)$ and the current input $x(k)$. This is because all the ``historical information'' priori to $k-1$ that is useful in calculating $y(k)$ is contained in $y(k-1)$. 

In general, if the current status of a system can be formulated as a function of the one-step previous status of the system and the current input and noise, the system model is known as a \textit{Markov process}. Markov process is widely used in modeling the system behavior. The famous state-space realization of a system is a systematic way of using a Markov process to model a system. 

By tracing back in time, we can find the ``initial state'' of a Markov process. In the context of \eqref{eq:diffrenceeqexp}, initial state can be denoted as $y(0)$. We do not care the values of $y(k)$ and $x(k)$ priori to $k=0$. From the recursive equation \eqref{eq:diffrenceeqexp}, it is obvious that $y(k)$ is essentially determined by the initial state $y(0)$ and the historical inputs $x(0), ..., x(k-1)$.

For the convenience of analysis, it is quite common that zero initial state (also known as relaxed initial state) $y(0)=0$ is assumed, which allows us to focus on the study of input-output relationship. The system response, in which case only affected by the inputs $x(0), ..., x(k-1)$, is known as the \textit{zero state response}. Likewise, there is the \textit{zero input response} of the system where non-zero initial state $y(0)\neq 0$ and zero input $x(k)=0$ are considered.
