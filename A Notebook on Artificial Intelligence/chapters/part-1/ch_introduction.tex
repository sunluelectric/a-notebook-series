\chapter{Introduction to Artificial Intelligence}

This chapter discusses the concepts and scope of \myabb{artificial intelligence}{AI} and gives a brief review of its development trend.

\section{Artificial Intelligence}

\mync{Artificial intelligence}[AI] refers to the intelligent entities we human have built to mimic ourselves, or part of ourselves. 

It is theoretically possible to explicitly program a rule-based system that can mimic human behaviors, but the realization will be too expensive and very likely not robust enough. Nowadays it has reached consensus that machine learning should be used to build AI.

\subsection{Machine Learning}

\mync{Machine learning} refers to the field of study that gives computers the ability to learn (from the data) without being explicitly programmed to do so. Compared with an explicitly programmed AI, machine learning is often less labor insensitive and more robust, but relies more heavily on the quality and quantity of the training data.

Depending on the labeling of samples, there are at least the following three types of machine learning problems.

\subsubsection*{Supervised Learning}

In \mync{supervised learning}, a training sample is given in the form of $(x,y)$, where $x$ is the input features (usually in vector form) and $y$ the label or reference. The purpose of supervised learning is to build a machine learning system that estimates $y$ when receiving input $x$.

There are broadly two types of supervised learning problems, depending on the format of $y$. If $y$ is a continuous value, the supervise learning problem is also known as a \mync{regression} problem which tries to mimic function $y = f(x)$. If $y$ is a discrete value taken from a finite set, the supervised learning problem is also known as a \mync{classification} problem which tries to find the most likely label for the corresponding input. 

Examples are given below.
\begin{itemize}
\item Regression: on a house price guide website, given the size and location of a house, estimate its price on the market.
\item Classification: given a picture of an animal, classify what animal it is from a finite set of possible animals.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{Reinforcement Learning}

In \mync{reinforcement learning}, a training sample is given in the form of $(x, a, r)$ where $x$ is the state of the system, $a$ (optional) an action that the system takes, and $r$ the reward or penalty. At any state, the system can take an action, transforms to a new state, and meantime gains some rewards. The purpose of reinforcement learning is to build a machine learning system that determines the optimal actions to take that maximizes the utility of the system. The utility often includes not only the immediate reward, but also future foreseeable benefits. 

Examples are given below. 
\begin{itemize}
\item In an autonomous driving vehicle, given the current status of the road environment and the status of the vehicle, decide what actions to take.
\item Given a board game (such as chess, go, etc.) and its current board configuration, decide the best next move.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{Unsupervised Learning}

In \mync{unsupervised learning}, a sample is given in the form of $(x)$, i.e., only the input corpus but not any label, reference or reward. The machine is expected to find the patterns hidden in $(x)$. The purpose of unsupervised learning is to build a machine learning system that categories a new input based on the detected patterns. 

Examples are given below.
\begin{itemize}
\item In a auto-fill text completion tool, given the first part of a sentence, fill in the remainder of the sentence.
\item Based on the movies a user has recently viewed, recommend new movies that they are likely to enjoy.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Scope of Artificial Intelligence}

It is not easy to draw a universally agreed line on what \myabb{artificial intelligence}{AI} should involve because our understanding of intelligence is evolving over time. 

We look at ourselves and try to find out what makes human intelligent. Many concepts, models and even research areas have been proposed to explain what human intelligence is composed of. For example, human has \mync{knowledge} which is a mechanism that allows us to remember facts and experience, and human can obtain new knowledge by either \mync{reasoning} which derives new knowledge from existing knowledge, or \mync{learning} which gets knowledge from examples, datasets or experiences. Human is usually \mync{rational} when making decisions, which means we do the ``right thing'' that is most beneficial to individuals or teams. Everything above, and probably many other factors that we are not aware of yet, jointly make human intelligent.

We want to build a system that can mimic human behaviors and complete human jobs with the same or better quality. Ideally, the system should have human-level intelligence or even beyond. This level of intelligence is often known as the \mync{artificial general intelligence}[AGI]. 

\mync{Turing test}, proposed by Alan Turing, is a widely accepted operational definition of (a portion of) AGI. A computer-based system passes the test if a human interrogator, after posing some questions, cannot tell whether the responses come from a computer or a human. Obviously, to pass the Turing test, the computer should be able to process natural languages, have knowledge representations, can do human-level reasoning, can make rational decisions, can learn from conversations, and can be generative and creative. A practically more useful AGI system should be able to process not just writing languages, but also audio and video.

As of this writing, we have not yet achieved AGI, although recent advent in \myabb{large language model}{LLM} (see Part IV of this notebook for more details) has for a short time made us believe that we might be close to it.

It is worth mentioning that although there is not yet a comprehensive AI that is comparable with a human at every aspect, we do have AIs that are very good at specific tasks, such as classifications, processing videos and audios, playing board games, autonomous driving, etc., and they can be better than the world champion in these tasks. They are not AGI, yet still useful and productive in practice, and have started playing important roles in the industry and the society.
